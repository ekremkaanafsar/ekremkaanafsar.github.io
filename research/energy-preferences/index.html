Preference Formation in Energy Production: A Survey of Wind and Oil/Gas Project Attitudes in the Northeast

The question

When energy development arrives in a community, what shapes how local landowners feel about it? This paper tests three explanations against each other: the externalities of the projects themselves, partisan cues, and simple proximity to development.

Setting and data

The Twin Tiers along the New York-Pennsylvania border are an unusual natural laboratory: wind energy and hydraulic fracturing grew there at the same time. We analyse an original mail survey of 354 residents of the region, together with energy, environmental and econometric data from 14 Twin Tiers counties.

Research design

Because counties differ in which kinds of development they host, the survey sets up a natural experiment. Residents are compared across counties with wind only, hydrocarbons only, both, and neither.

We model attitudes toward each type of development with ordered and multinomial logit models, alongside t-tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. All statistical analyses are run in Stata and R.

Key findings

01

Partisanship significantly predicts attitudes toward oil and gas development, but not toward wind energy.

02

Proximity matters more for hydrocarbons than for wind: county-type effects are stronger for oil and gas development than for wind.

My role

  • Co-leading the survey research with Prof. Robert A. Holahan as a Research Assistant at Binghamton University (since January 2024).
  • Designed, fielded and analysed the 354-respondent survey.
  • Collected and analysed the county-level energy, environmental and econometric data, using ordered logit, multinomial logit, t-tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.
  • I run all of the statistical analyses in Stata and R.
